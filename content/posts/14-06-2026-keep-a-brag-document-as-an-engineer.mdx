It's review season. Your manager asks what you accomplished this year, and your mind goes blank past the last sprint. You shipped a lot—but you can only picture the thing you merged on Tuesday.

That's not a memory problem. It's a **recency bias** problem: at evaluation time, everyone—you and your manager—remembers the last few weeks and quietly forgets the other fifty. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple. **Keep a brag document.**

A brag document is just a running list of what you did and why it mattered. I keep one as a senior frontend engineer in fintech, and it has done more for my career conversations than any productivity app I've tried. Here's how to start one in the next ten minutes.

## What a brag document actually is

It's a single file you append to over time. Not a journal of feelings—a record of **impact**, with links as evidence.

Each entry answers three questions: what did you do, what changed because of it, and where's the proof. The proof is the part most people skip and the part that wins promotions. A merged PR, a design doc, a dashboard showing the bug rate dropping, a thread where you unblocked a teammate—these are concrete artifacts your manager can cite when they argue your case in a room you're not in.

The term comes from engineer Julia Evans, who popularized the idea that **your work doesn't speak for itself—you have to speak for it.** That feels uncomfortable if you were raised to think good work gets noticed automatically. It doesn't. Visibility is a separate skill from competence, and a brag doc is how you build it without becoming the person who narrates every commit in Slack.

## Start native: one plain Markdown file

Resist the urge to shop for a tool. You do not need Notion, a template marketplace, or a "career OS." **A single Markdown file in a private repo or your notes app is the whole system.** Plain text means it's searchable, diffable, portable between jobs, and never locked behind someone's subscription.

Here's the entire structure I use:

```markdown
# Brag Doc — 2026

## Q2

### Shipped
- Rebuilt the payments confirmation flow in Next.js.
  Cut the abandoned-payment rate on that screen.
  → PR #482, design doc, before/after funnel chart

### Helped others
- Paired with two engineers on our Intl money-formatting
  approach; wrote it up so the team stopped reinventing it.
  → internal doc, thread

### Learned / leveled up
- Owned my first cross-team technical decision (currency
  rounding rules) end to end.
  → ADR #12
```

Three buckets—**shipped, helped others, learned**—because promotion rubrics reward more than just code. Helping teammates and growing your scope are exactly what separates a senior from a mid-level engineer, and they're the entries you'll otherwise never remember.

## The 10-minute weekly habit

The document only works if it's current, and it only stays current if updating it is frictionless. So make it a tiny recurring ritual, not a project.

**Block ten minutes every Friday.** Open the file, skim your week—merged PRs, reviews you gave, meetings where you actually moved something, the moment you helped someone get unstuck—and jot a line or two per item. Don't polish. Bullet points with a link are enough; you're capturing raw material, not writing the performance review yet.

One upgrade that compounds: **also log the things that annoyed you this week**—the flaky test, the slow build, the confusing onboarding doc. Review that list monthly and fix the top one. Over a year that habit quietly removes the friction that's been taxing your whole team, and "I made our CI 40% faster" is a brag-doc entry in its own right.

If you're aiming for the next level, write entries in the language of that level. Targeting lead or staff? Note where a piece of work showed technical direction, mentorship, or impact beyond your own tickets. Done consistently, the document becomes evidence you're *already* operating one rung up—which is exactly the case your manager needs to make.

## Where a tool finally earns its place

Native-first doesn't mean tool-never. There's one moment a tool pulls its weight: **turning twelve months of bullets into a tight summary at review time.**

When you've got a year of dense notes, pasting them into an AI assistant and asking it to group them into themes and draft a first-pass summary is a genuine time-saver. That's the line where it earns its place—synthesis of material you already captured. It is *not* a substitute for the weekly habit. AI can summarize a year of evidence; it cannot invent the evidence. If the file is empty in November, no tool saves you.

## The short version

Memory fails you at exactly the wrong moment, so **keep a brag document**: a single plain-Markdown file, three buckets—**shipped, helped others, learned**—with a **link as proof** on every entry. Spend **ten minutes each Friday** topping it up, and log your weekly annoyances so you can fix the biggest one each month. Write entries in the language of the level you want next. Reach for an **AI tool only at the end**, to summarize a year you already recorded—never to replace the habit itself.

Start the file today, while you still remember this week. Your next self, sitting in a review with a blank mind, will thank you.
