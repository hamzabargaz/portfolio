Do you actually *know* AI is making you faster? Or does it just feel that way?

It turns out those are very different things. In mid-2025, the research nonprofit METR ran a careful [study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/): experienced developers did real tasks, some with AI and some without, so the two could be compared fairly. The developers expected AI to make them 24% faster. It made them **19% slower**. And even after finishing, they still believed it had sped them up.

**Feeling fast and being fast are not the same.** That gap is the trap.

## The Tools Got Better. The Trap Didn't Go Away.

To be fair, that study used early-2025 tools, and they've improved a lot since. A [2026 follow-up](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) with newer AI agents did point to a real speedup — about 18%. But the researchers warn the result is shaky, because many developers now refuse to work without AI at all, which skews who takes part.

One of them said it well: working without AI now feels *"like trying to walk across the city when you're used to taking an Uber."*

So what's the honest takeaway from the research so far? **AI probably does help. The boost is real, but smaller than it feels — and your own sense of speed is the least trustworthy measure you have.**

## Why Your Gut Gets This Wrong

When an AI writes 200 lines while you grab a coffee, that feels like magic. What your brain forgets is the 40 minutes afterward spent reviewing it, fixing a subtly wrong piece, and asking again.

Effort feels like work. Waiting and reviewing don't — even though they burn the same hours.

I feel this in fintech, where the review bar is non-negotiable: money screens don't get to ship code that's "mostly right." The AI's draft shows up fast. *Trustworthy* code shows up only as fast as I can check it. The slow part moved — it didn't disappear.

## Measure Yourself Before the Vibes Do

You don't need a fancy tool for this. Your git history is already the data.

```bash
# Your merged PRs in the last 4 weeks
git log --since="4 weeks ago" --author="you@email.com" \
  --merges --oneline | wc -l
```

Each week, track two or three plain numbers: how many tasks you shipped, how long they took from start to merge, and how often the AI's output survived review without rework. A paid dashboard only earns its place once doing this by hand costs more than the subscription.

Then run your own tiny test: for two weeks, do similar tasks — some with heavy AI help, some without — and write down the real clock time. It's rough, but it beats guessing. METR's developers were off by nearly 40 points.

## Habits That Keep the Gains Real

**Choose tasks on purpose.** AI is great at fresh scaffolding, tests, and unfamiliar APIs. It's weakest in large, mature codebases with strict unwritten rules. Know which one you're in before you hand work off.

**Don't babysit the agent.** Half-watching it work is the worst of both worlds — no focus, no rest. Start the task, then give something else your full attention until it's done.

**Review it like a junior's work.** Because that's what it is: fast, confident, and occasionally wrong in ways that look right.

**Keep one AI-free block a week.** Debugging instinct and design judgment are muscles. The Uber line should worry you a little — convenience is great, but you still want to be able to walk.

## The Short Version

AI makes coding *feel* faster than it often is — METR's developers felt 20% quicker while actually being 19% slower. Newer tools do help, but self-reports stay unreliable, so measure the real thing: tasks shipped, time-to-merge, and rework rate, all from the git history you already have. Hand off boilerplate and greenfield work; stay hands-on in high-stakes code. Stop babysitting agents, review their output like a junior's, and keep one AI-free block a week to stay sharp.
